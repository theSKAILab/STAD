Abstract

The Spatial Temporal Aggregate Data (STAD) ontology provides a machine-interpretable representation of the semantics of statistically aggregated spatial and temporal data. Published aggregate data often lack explicit information about how values were computed, which source data were used, and the spatial and temporal conditions under which the values apply. STAD addresses this limitation by representing four central aspects of an aggregate datapoint: its base data, transformation method, spatial support (spatial coverage), and temporal support (temporal coverage and aggregation period), together with its quality kind and value. It provides a reusable ontology built around this core ontology design pattern, with refinements specifically targeting spatial and temporal aggregates. The ontology supports semantically describing other important concepts and relationships closely related to aggregate datapoints, including datasets, datapoints (including quality kinds and values), spatial and temporal resolution, and computational transformation kinds. Implemented as a reusable OWL 2 ontology, STAD supports the consistent publication, discovery, comparison, integration, and querying of aggregated data across heterogeneous datasets and knowledge graphs.

Introduction back to ToC

Advances in data collection technologies have made large volumes of spatial and temporal data available across environmental science, climate research, ecology, public health, and many other domains. Such data are frequently aggregated using operations such as the arithmetic mean, minimum, maximum, or percentile. Aggregation can reduce storage and processing requirements, reveal broader patterns, facilitate decision-making, and protect sensitive information. Nevertheless, an aggregate value cannot be interpreted reliably from its label alone. Two values described as “daily mean temperature,” for example, may have been calculated from 24 hourly observations, from minute-level observations, or from only the daily minimum and maximum. Similarly, two seasonal aggregates may differ in the periods regarded as belonging to the season, the spatial areas they represent, the resolution of their source data, or the transformations used to compute them. Without explicit descriptions of these characteristics, aggregate data can be difficult to discover, compare, integrate, and reuse correctly. The Spatial Temporal Aggregate Data (STAD) ontology addresses this problem by making aggregation semantics explicit and machine interpretable. STAD provides an ontology design pattern for characterizing aggregate datapoints through their base data, transformation method, spatial support, and temporal support. It extends this pattern into a broader semantic framework for datasets, datapoints, values, quality kinds, resolution, and computational provenance. STAD is intended for use across domains in which spatially or temporally aggregated data must be represented, queried, or compared.

Spatial and Temporal Aggregate Data (STAD) Ontology: Overview back to ToC

STAD consists of a core ontology and a complementary computational provenance module called STAD-MLS. The core ontology uses the namespace http://purl.org/spatialai/stad/v2/core#, while STAD-MLS uses http://purl.org/spatialai/stad/v2/mls#. The ontologies are formalized in OWL 2 RL and are designed for direct reuse in RDF knowledge graphs.

The general data model centers on the class Data, with Datapoint and Dataset as its principal subclasses. A Datapoint represents an atomic unit of data and is associated with a QualityKind describing what it represents and a DataValue recording its value. Datapoints may be quantitative or qualitative and may result from observation or computation. A Dataset groups datapoints and may be associated with a DatasetDescription containing metadata about the dataset, including its spatial and temporal resolution.

StatisticalAggregateDatapoint represents a datapoint obtained by statistically transforming other data. STAD distinguishes SpatialAggregateDatapoint, TemporalAggregateDatapoint, and SpatioTemporalAggregateDatapoint according to whether the aggregation has spatial support, temporal support, or both. Each statistical aggregate can be characterized along four principal semantic dimensions:

  • Base data identify the source data from which the aggregate was derived. They may be represented as individual datapoints or as an entire dataset.
  • DataTransformation describes the kind of method that was used to compute the aggregate. A transformation may be represented at a general level, such as an arithmetic mean, minimum, or maximum, or described in greater computational detail through STAD-MLS.
  • Spatial support identifies the spatial extent, represented as SpatialCoverage over which the base data were aggregated and for which the resulting value is meaningful. Spatial coverage may be represented using GeoSPARQL features and geometries.
  • Temporal support describes when the aggregate applies. STAD distinguishes TemporalCoverage, representing the overall temporal extent of an aggregate, from its AggregationPeriod, representing the potentially discontinuous portions of that extent from which observations were actually included.

Spatial and temporal resolution are modeled separately from spatial and temporal support. Both ResolutionDescriptors characterizes the granularity or sampling structure of a base dataset and is therefore recorded in its DatasetDescription. SpatialResolution can describe characteristics such as raster-cell size, spacing between observation locations, sampling density, or coverage ratio. TemporalResolution can describe a regular sampling interval or a statistical summary of irregular temporal spacing.

The optional STAD-MLS extension supports more detailed descriptions of computational provenance. It distinguishes an algorithm from its implementation and from a particular execution of that implementation. An execution can be linked to its inputs, outputs, and parameter settings, enabling aggregates to be documented at the level needed for reproducibility. When such detail is unavailable or unnecessary, an aggregate may instead be linked directly to a more general transformation kind.

STAD reuses and aligns with established semantic standards. It uses OWL-Time to describe temporal entities and temporal aggregates and selected GeoSPARQL terms to describe spatial features and geometries. Quantitative values may be represented using QUDT, OM, or another suitable quantities-and-units ontology. This modular design allows STAD to express aggregation semantics at different levels of detail and to complement the domain ontologies already used by a dataset or knowledge graph.
